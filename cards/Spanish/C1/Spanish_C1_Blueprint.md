# Spanish (Latin American Standard) — C1 Sentence-Mining Blueprint

## 1. Total Scope Assessment

**Target total: 2,900 sentences, in 42 batches (41 content batches + 1 mixed-review/integration batch).**

Justification:
- **Vocabulary**: C1 adds ~1,800–2,200 new lemmas plus a large expansion of collocational and idiomatic range (cumulative ~6,000–7,000 lexical items/expressions, per PCIC/CEFR C1 benchmarks including phraseology). At C1 density (long, stylistically varied sentences with 2–3 embedded idioms/collocations each), ~2,200–2,300 sentences are needed for cumulative coverage.
- **Grammar/syntax load**: Core grammar is largely complete by C1; the remaining difficulty is stylistic and pragmatic — subtle mood selection in near-equivalent contexts, literary/journalistic syntactic inversion, ellipsis, and cohesion devices that operate above the sentence level. This shifts weight from "new rules" to "high-density stylistic pattern training," which the design principles flag as requiring extra density.
- **Extra density**: register and genre-specific batches (literary, journalistic, academic, legal-formal, rhetorical) multiply the batch count because each genre has distinct syntactic conventions that must be modeled separately rather than folded into general grammar batches.
- 2,900 ÷ 42 batches gives batches of ~50–75 sentences, within the clean-generation window.

## 3. Linguistic Justification

**Subtle mood selection** (Batches 2–4) is the true center of gravity at C1: by this stage learners already "know the rules" of the subjunctive, but native-like competence requires resolving genuinely ambiguous or speaker-stance-dependent cases (e.g., aunque with indicative signaling the concession is accepted as fact vs. subjunctive signaling it as hypothetical). This cannot be taught as a rule; it requires large sets of near-minimal-pair sentences, which is why three full batches are dedicated to it rather than folding it into a single "subjunctive review."

**Cohesion and stylistic batches** (5–16) reflect that C1 Spanish difficulty has shifted from clause-internal grammar to text-level organization: ellipsis, anaphora, cleft constructions, and reformulation markers are what separate a grammatically correct learner from a stylistically fluent one, and Spanish deploys these more assertively than English in formal registers (e.g., heavier use of nominal style and absolute constructions).

**Genre-specific batches** (17–24, 33) are proportionally the largest single block because C1 descriptors explicitly require comprehension and production across specialized and literary text types, each of which has distinct syntactic fingerprints in Spanish — legal Spanish's archaic subordination, journalism's compressed quotation conventions, and literary prose's free indirect discourse cannot be taught generically without diluting authenticity.

**Cross-genre synthesis batches** (39–40) are a C1-specific design choice: presenting the same semantic content across multiple registers side-by-side is one of the most efficient ways to make register choice explicit and conscious, directly targeting the "appropriateness" dimension of the C1 descriptor rather than only grammatical accuracy.

**Long-sentence parsing** (31–32) is pushed to paragraph-length single sentences here, reflecting that authentic formal/literary Spanish routinely produces sentences of a length and embedding depth that would be considered run-on in English — this is a genuine typological difference in written register norms, not a stylistic quirk, and requires dedicated high-volume exposure.
