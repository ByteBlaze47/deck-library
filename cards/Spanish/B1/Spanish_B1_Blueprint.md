# Spanish (Latin American Standard) — B1 Sentence-Mining Blueprint

## 1. Total Scope Assessment

**Target total: 1,750 sentences, in 32 batches (31 content batches + 1 mixed-review/integration batch).**

Justification:
- **Vocabulary**: B1 adds ~800–1,000 new lemmas (cumulative ~2,000, per PCIC/CEFR B1 benchmarks), including abstract nouns, opinion/argumentation vocabulary, and domain vocabulary (work, media, environment, technology). At B1 sentence complexity (subordinate clauses, multiple lemmas per sentence), ~1,300–1,400 sentences are needed for full cumulative vocabulary coverage.
- **Grammar/syntax load**: B1 is where the present subjunctive becomes fully productive (not just fixed triggers), past subjunctive is introduced, the perfect-tense system is completed (pluperfect), reported speech and the passive/impersonal "se" system appear, and si-clause conditionals begin. Each of these is a genuinely new syntactic subsystem requiring dedicated batches, not incidental exposure — this justifies roughly 400+ sentences beyond pure vocabulary need.
- **Extra density**: sequence-of-tense rules between subjunctive clauses and main clauses, plus reported-speech tense backshifting, are long-distance dependency phenomena that require many contrastive examples to internalize.
- 1,750 ÷ 32 batches gives batches of ~40–60 sentences, within the clean-generation window.

## 3. Linguistic Justification

**The subjunctive mood's productive expansion** is the central challenge of B1 Spanish. Unlike A2's fixed-phrase introduction, B1 requires learners to generalize the subject-change + trigger-category rule across doubt, emotion, purpose, indefinite antecedents, and temporal/concessive connectors. The blueprint isolates each trigger category into its own batch (2–6) rather than teaching "the subjunctive" as one topic, because each category has distinct semantic licensing conditions and Spanish speakers select among them based on subtle pragmatic cues that require category-specific repetition to acquire.

**Sequence-of-tense agreement** between subjunctive clauses and their main clauses (Batch 14) and **tense backshifting in reported speech** (Batch 8) are long-distance dependency phenomena: the correct verb form in the subordinate clause depends on a verb form several words away in the main clause. These require dedicated batches because they are a common fossilization point even at higher levels if not explicitly isolated.

**The -ra/-se past subjunctive alternation** (Batch 13) is a Latin-American-standard-specific note: both forms are used, but -ra is dominant in speech while -se appears more in formal/literary register — this register-conditioned free variation is unique to Spanish and needs explicit contrastive treatment.

**Se-construction ambiguity** (Batch 11) — where "se vende" can be impersonal, passive, or reflexive depending on context — is a genuine parsing challenge even for advanced learners and is isolated because it requires many disambiguating context sentences rather than a single rule.

**Register split (Batches 28–29)** is elevated at B1 because learners are now expected to produce connected discourse, and Latin American Spanish shows significant formal/colloquial divergence (vocabulary, connectors, diminutive use) that must be modeled side-by-side to prevent register-blindness in production.
