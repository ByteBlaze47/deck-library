# Spanish (Latin American Standard) — A1 Sentence-Mining Blueprint

## 1. Total Scope Assessment

**Target total: 950 sentences, in 24 batches (23 content batches + 1 mixed-review/integration batch).**

Justification:
- **Vocabulary**: A1 standard coverage is ~500–600 lemmas (CEFR/Instituto Cervantes PCIC "A1" plan curricular). At ~1.5–2 new/recycled target lemmas per sentence embedded in natural context, full cumulative coverage with enough repetition (3–5 exposures per lemma, required for retention) needs roughly 900–1,000 sentences.
- **Grammar/syntax load**: A1 Spanish has a deceptively large functional grammar load for a "beginner" level compared to English: ser/estar split, grammatical gender agreement propagating through articles/adjectives/pronouns, four second-person register forms in the Americas (tú, usted, ustedes, and dialectal vos-awareness), verb conjugation for 3 regular classes × irregular stem/root changes, clitic pronoun placement, and gustar-type inverted-subject verbs. Each of these needs its own dedicated batch plus recurring reinforcement across thematic batches — this alone justifies 350–400 sentences beyond pure vocabulary needs.
- **Extra density for language-specific traps**: Spanish A1 already forces learners into agreement chains (article+noun+adjective+possible participle), clitic pronoun word order, and register bifurcation (tú vs. usted) that don't exist at A1 in many other languages. These require dedicated "structural map" batches early on and recurring drilling batches later, adding density beyond a generic A1 count.
- 950 sentences ÷ ~24 batches gives batches of 30–45 sentences each, within the clean-generation window.

## 3. Linguistic Justification

**Ser/estar split** is the single most persistent A1 trap for Spanish, since English collapses both into "to be." Rather than teach it once, the blueprint installs it in two graded passes (Batch 3: traits/identity vs. states; Batch 15: location mastery) and then re-tests it silently inside nearly every later thematic batch, so the distinction is trained through repeated contextual disambiguation rather than a single rule dump.

**Gender/number agreement chains** are a structural feature absent from English: every noun phrase can require up to four elements (article, noun, adjective, sometimes participle) to agree simultaneously. Batches 2, 4, and 6 deliberately front-load noun-phrase-heavy content (descriptions, family, objects) so agreement becomes automatic before verb morphology complexity is added.

**Register bifurcation (tú vs. usted, and by extension vos-awareness in some LatAm regions)** has no parallel in English A1 instruction and is easy to under-teach. The blueprint dedicates Batch 1 to installing the map early and Batch 22 to drilling the full parallel formal conjugation system later, preventing learners from defaulting to only the informal register.

**Clitic pronoun behavior** (reflexives in Batch 10, indirect objects with gustar-type verbs in Batch 12) requires mastering pre-verbal pronoun placement, a word-order pattern that is typologically unfamiliar to English speakers and a common source of fossilized errors if not isolated early and reinforced.

**Root-changing and high-frequency irregular verbs** (Batches 8–9) are grouped separately from regular conjugation because Spanish concentrates irregularity in exactly the verbs learners need most (tener, ir, hacer, querer, poder, decir), so isolating them prevents interference with the regular-pattern automatization built in Batch 7.

**Discourse-level coordination** (Batch 23) and the **cumulative integration batch** (24) exist because A1 Spanish learners are commonly left unable to produce anything beyond single clauses; explicitly training clause-linking connectors and then forcing multi-structure recombination closes the gap between "knows the grammar" and "can produce a real sentence."
