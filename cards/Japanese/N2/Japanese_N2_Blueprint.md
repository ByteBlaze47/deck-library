# Japanese (Tokyo Standard) — N2 Sentence-Based Curriculum Blueprint

## 1. Total Scope Assessment

N2 targets **~6,000 cumulative vocabulary items**, **~1,000 kanji**, and approximately **200 new grammar patterns**, most of them drawn from **written/journalistic Japanese, formal business correspondence, and abstract logical argumentation** rather than everyday conversation. This is the level where Japanese grammar diverges most sharply from conversational patterns learners already know.

Because N2 patterns are heavily register-dependent (a pattern natural in a newspaper editorial may sound stilted in speech, and vice versa), each pattern needs paired sentence sets (formal-context example + explicit register note), pushing exposure density to 6–8 sentences per point.

**Target: ~1,900 sentences**, organized into **34 batches**, capped at 55 sentences each.

## 3. Linguistic Justification

- **Grammar-as-register, not grammar-as-meaning:** Most N2 patterns don't introduce new *meanings* so much as new *registers* for meanings already known (e.g., ~にもかかわらず vs. the N3 ~のに both mean "despite," but the former is exclusively written/formal). Batch 1 exists specifically to pre-establish this register map so every subsequent batch can reference it rather than re-explaining formality each time.
- **Near-untranslatable emphasis particles (さえ/すら/だに):** These form an escalating scale of "even" with no single English word covering all three, and native speakers select among them based on subtle rhetorical weight — a distinction that cannot be taught through definition alone and requires the large 40-sentence contrastive batch (25).
- **Sino-Japanese (kango) compound density:** At N2, sentence subjects and objects are increasingly dense two-and-three-kanji compounds functioning as single lexical units (rather than the native-Japanese, kana-heavy phrasing of N4/N5). Batch 30 is dedicated to training parsing speed for this shift, which is a distinct skill from vocabulary recognition.
- **Long-sentence parsing:** N2 reading passages routinely embed 3–4 subordinate clauses in a single verb-final sentence — a structural pattern absent from English, where subordinate clauses can be freely reordered. Batch 33 isolates this parsing skill separately from new-grammar acquisition.
- **Written correspondence formulas:** Japanese business/formal writing uses fixed opening/closing phrases (拝啓, 敬具, etc.) that function as ritualized units rather than compositional grammar — these are taught as fixed chunks (Batch 23) rather than decomposed grammatically, mirroring how native speakers acquire them.
