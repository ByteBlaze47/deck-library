\*\*1. Total Scope Assessment\*\*



Target total: \*\*1,450–1,600 sentences\*\*.



Justification:  

Standard N5 coverage requires roughly 800 core vocabulary items and approximately 80 grammar/syntactic patterns (particles, verb conjugations across groups, adjective conjugations, existence constructions, basic request/permission/prohibition forms, comparisons, temporal expressions, and discourse connectors). Japanese-specific challenges at this level—especially the particle/case system (は vs が, に vs で, を, へ, と, も, の, から/まで), register divergence (丁寧語 vs plain forms), te-form chaining, progressive/state ている, and early compounding—demand denser exposure than a simple “one example per point” list.  



A high-density sentence-mining approach therefore needs multiple controlled embeddings of each pattern inside coherent situations (3–6 sentences per major structure on average), progressive lengthening of dependency chains, and distributed vocabulary recycling across themes rather than isolated word lists. 1,450–1,600 sentences provide full coverage of the official/standard N5 inventory while remaining practical for later AI generation in small batches and for deliberate exposure/review cycles. This range avoids both under-exposure (insufficient mastery of particle contrasts and register shifts) and dilution (overly long or thematically scattered batches).

\*\*3. Linguistic Justification\*\*



Japanese (Tokyo Standard) at N5 is deceptively difficult because the surface simplicity of short sentences conceals several interlocking systems that English speakers (and many other L1 groups) find non-intuitive:



\- \*\*Particle/case system\*\*: は marks topic (often old or contrastive information), が marks focused subject or new information/existence; に vs で distinguish static location/goal from dynamic place-of-action; を, へ, と, も, の, から/まで each carry multiple functions that interact. Early isolated drills fail; the architecture therefore embeds these contrasts repeatedly inside coherent situations (existence, movement, preferences, routines) so the learner experiences the functional difference rather than memorizing labels.

\- \*\*Register divergence\*\*: Polite ます/です forms dominate textbooks and the test, yet real Tokyo speech rapidly shifts to plain forms. Batch 26 and progressive recycling force controlled switching so the learner does not treat polite and plain as separate languages.

\- \*\*Verb morphology \& chaining\*\*: Godan/ichidan/irregular classification, て-form multipurpose use (request, progressive, sequence, permission), and early compounding create long-distance dependencies even at N5. Dedicated batches for progressive aspect, sequence, and multi-te chaining (plus later long-sentence parsing) train the learner to hold and resolve these dependencies rather than process word-by-word.

\- \*\*Adjective and existence asymmetries\*\*: い- vs な-adjectives conjugate differently; あります vs います encode animacy; がほしい and ～たい are not simple “want” equivalents. Thematic embedding (preferences, health, objects) surfaces these repeatedly.

\- \*\*Dense compounding and discourse scaffolding\*\*: N5 already contains productive noun compounds and basic connectors (から, ので, けど, そして). Later batches deliberately increase density and sentence length so the learner practices the characteristic “left-branching + particle scaffolding” syntax of Japanese before moving to N4.



By keeping grammar almost never isolated and distributing the 800-word vocabulary across situational themes, the blueprint ensures every batch simultaneously advances communicative usefulness and the precise structural traps of Tokyo-standard Japanese at N5. The final mixed-review batch forces cumulative recombination, the critical step that converts controlled exposure into robust recognition and production.
