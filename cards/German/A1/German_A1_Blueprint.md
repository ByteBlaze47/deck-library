# German (Standard High German) — A1 Sentence-Based Curriculum Blueprint

## 1. Total Scope Assessment

A1 corresponds to roughly **500–750 active vocabulary items** (Goethe-Institut A1 wordlist ≈ 650 words) and a narrow grammatical core: present tense, basic modal verbs, the accusative/dative case split, separable verbs, and the Perfekt tense. To drill each vocabulary item in at least one meaningful sentence, plus repeat high-frequency grammar patterns (verb conjugation, case marking, word order) across multiple contexts, the estimated total requirement is:

**≈ 835 sentences**, divided into 14 topic batches.

This count is deliberately higher than the raw vocabulary count because German A1's main difficulty isn't lexical, it's structural: learners need repeated exposure to verb-second (V2) word order, case-marked articles (der/die/das → den/dem), and separable-verb splitting before these become automatic.

## 3. Linguistic Justification

German A1 presents several structural traps this batching is designed to isolate and drill separately rather than all at once:

- **Verb-second (V2) word order**: the finite verb must occupy the second syntactic position regardless of what comes first, which is unlike English and needs repeated exposure across varied sentence openers (Batches 1, 4, 8).
- **Case marking on articles**: nominative vs. accusative vs. dative changes the article form (der→den→dem), not just word meaning. Batches 3, 5, and 6 isolate each case before combining them.
- **Separable-prefix verbs**: prefixes like `auf-`, `an-`, `mit-` detach and move to clause-final position in main clauses (`Ich stehe auf`), a purely German structural quirk drilled in Batch 4.
- **Two-way prepositions**: prepositions like `in`, `an`, `auf` take accusative for motion and dative for location — the same preposition, different case, different meaning nuance (Batch 7).
- **Verb-final order in subordinate clauses**: once `weil`, `dass`, or `wenn` appears, the conjugated verb jumps to the end of the clause, directly contradicting the V2 rule just learned (Batch 11).
- **Perfekt tense auxiliary choice**: motion/change-of-state verbs take `sein`, most others take `haben` — a lexically governed distinction with no English equivalent, isolated in Batch 13.
